# Academic literature review for the Keystone Intelligence Engine

**The DPVI architecture is empirically well-supported by 2024–2026 research, but three findings demand design changes: naive debate adds almost nothing beyond voting, LLM evaluators systematically prefer style over substance, and self-improvement saturates after 2–3 iterations without new failure signals.** Across 50+ papers with empirical results, the six-layer pipeline (Specification → Parallel Research → Deliberation → Structuring → Evaluation → Self-Improvement) aligns with frontier research on multi-agent coordination, but each layer requires specific architectural refinements the literature now makes clear. The most consequential finding is that quality enforcement through structure—Keystone's core thesis—is validated by Google DeepMind's discovery that unstructured agent networks amplify errors **17.2×**, while centralized verification reduces this to **4.4×**.

---

## Multi-agent deliberation works, but only with architectural sophistication

The deliberation layer faces a paradox: the foundational Du et al. "LLM Debate" paper (ICML 2024; GitHub: `composable-models/llm_multiagent_debate`) showed multi-agent debate reduces hallucinations and improves factuality across six benchmarks. Yet the NeurIPS 2025 Spotlight "Debate or Vote" (Choi, Zhu, Li; arXiv:2508.17536; GitHub: `deeplearning-wisc/debate-or-vote`) proved that **majority voting alone accounts for most performance gains typically attributed to debate**. Debate induces a *martingale* over agents' belief trajectories—it does not systematically improve expected correctness without targeted structural interventions like anti-conformist agents or explicit correction biases.

Self-MoA (Li et al., ICLR 2025; arXiv:2502.00674) sharpened this finding: a single strong model with self-ensembling outperforms mixed-model ensembles by **6.6% on AlpacaEval 2.0** and 3.8% across MMLU, CRUX, and MATH. Agent quality dominates agent diversity. Wu et al.'s controlled study "Can LLM Agents Really Debate?" (arXiv:2511.07784) confirmed that **intrinsic reasoning strength and group diversity are the dominant drivers**, while structural parameters (debate order, confidence visibility) offer limited gains. Critically, MAD cannot exceed the accuracy of its strongest participant, and visible confidence scores create over-confidence cascades.

Three papers transform this from a negative to a conditional positive. **Attention-MoA** (Wen et al., January 2026; arXiv:2601.16596) introduced inter-agent semantic attention and residual synthesis, achieving **91.15% LC Win Rate on AlpacaEval 2.0**—small open-source models (12B–32B) beating Claude-4.5-Sonnet and GPT-4.1. The "Breaking Mental Set" paper (DMAD, Liu et al., ICLR 2025; GitHub: `MraDonkey/DMAD`) proved that **methodological diversity beats persona diversity**: assigning agents different reasoning methods (CoT, step-back prompting, compositional reasoning) rather than just different roles produces consistent gains over standard debate. A-HMAD (Zhou & Chen, 2025) achieved 4–6% accuracy improvement through heterogeneous agent roles with a learned consensus module replacing majority voting, reducing factual errors by over **30%** in biography generation.

The "Society of Thought" paper (Kim, Lai, Scherrer, Agüera y Arcas, Evans; arXiv:2601.10825, January 2026) provided the theoretical anchor: advanced reasoning models like DeepSeek-R1 spontaneously simulate internal multi-agent debate during reasoning. Models fine-tuned on multi-party conversation data significantly outperformed standard chain-of-thought monologues. Evans called explicitly for architectures incorporating "brainstorming, devil's advocacy, constructive conflict as designed features." The Mixture-of-Agents paper (Together AI, ICLR 2025 Spotlight) validated layered architectures, achieving **65.1% on AlpacaEval 2.0 vs. 57.5% by GPT-4 Omni** using only open-source models—confirming that even lower-quality auxiliary perspectives improve the aggregator.

**Net conclusion for deliberation:** Multi-agent deliberation validates Keystone's architecture *if* it implements: (1) the strongest available model as primary backbone with temperature-varied sampling, (2) genuine methodological diversity across agents (quantitative analysis, qualitative framing, adversarial critique—not just persona labels), (3) structured critique-and-refine with semantic attention between agents, (4) a dedicated anti-conformist agent, (5) validity-weighted consensus rather than majority voting, (6) one deliberation pass with adaptive early stopping, and (7) hidden confidence scores between agents. Naive round-robin debate should be replaced entirely. This **extends** the current architectural assumptions.

## The evaluator must decompose dimensions and use diverse judge ensembles

SOS-Bench (Feuer et al., ICLR 2025; arXiv:2409.15268; GitHub: `penfever/sos-bench`) established the evaluator's central challenge: **LLM judges systematically prefer style over factual accuracy and safety**. Factor analysis showed three factors explain 65% of variance in GPT-4o judgments, with style dominating. The CALM framework (Ye et al., ICLR 2025; arXiv:2410.02736; GitHub: `Y0oMu/LLM-Judge-Bias-Dataset`) catalogued **12 distinct bias types** including position bias, verbosity bias, fallacy-oversight (ignoring logical errors), authority bias (inflated credibility from citations regardless of evidence quality), and self-enhancement bias. Even GPT-4-class models remain vulnerable across multiple bias types.

The "When AIs Judge AIs" survey (Yu, August 2025; arXiv:2508.02994) quantified a critical ceiling: **SME–LLM judge agreement in expert domains is only 60–68%**, far below the 80–90% Spearman correlation on generic tasks. For consulting-quality research evaluation, generic LLM judges are insufficient. Nonsense responses can trick GPT-4 into high rankings when crafted persuasively.

Ensemble approaches consistently outperform single judges. **PoLL** (Verga et al., Cohere, 2024; arXiv:2404.18796) demonstrated a panel of three smaller models from different families outperforms a single GPT-4 judge while being **7× cheaper** and exhibiting less intra-model bias. **SE-Jury** (ASE 2025; arXiv:2505.20854) achieved **29.6–140.8% improvement** over existing metrics through five evaluation strategies with dynamic team selection that matched full-ensemble performance while cutting cost by ~50%. MAJ-EVAL (Chen et al., July 2025; arXiv:2507.21028) reached **Spearman ρ of 0.47** versus 0.15–0.36 for single-judge baselines by extracting stakeholder personas from domain literature and having them debate before scoring.

Prometheus 2 (Kim et al., EMNLP 2024; GitHub: `prometheus-eval/prometheus-eval`) provides the open-source evaluator backbone: 7B and 8x7B models achieving 0.6–0.7 Pearson correlation with GPT-4 on direct assessment and 72–85% agreement with humans on pairwise ranking, supporting custom evaluation rubrics. Agent-as-a-Judge (Zhuge et al., October 2024; arXiv:2410.10934) showed that agent-evaluators dramatically outperform static LLM judges by running code, querying databases, and independently verifying results—matching human evaluation reliability. DeepResearchGym (CMU, arXiv:2505.19253; website: deepresearchgym.ai) provides a reproducible evaluation sandbox with multi-dimensional metrics (coverage, faithfulness, quality) achieving human agreement rates exceeding inter-annotator agreement.

Two additional papers shape evaluator design. EvalPlanner (Saha et al., January 2025; arXiv:2501.18099) achieved **SOTA 93.9 on RewardBench** by decoupling evaluation into planning (generating criteria and rubric steps) then execution. "Trust or Escalate" (ICLR 2025) guarantees target human agreement levels while keeping most evaluations automated, with cascading from weak to strong judges saving **up to 78.5%** evaluation cost. The Rubrics as Rewards paper (Gunjal et al., Scale AI, NeurIPS 2025 Workshop) achieved up to **28% improvement** on HealthBench using structured, prompt-specific checklists with categorical weights (Essential/Important/Optional/Pitfall) instead of opaque reward models.

**Net conclusion for evaluation:** The evaluator design is validated as the most important component, but must implement: dimension decomposition (factual accuracy, analytical rigor, completeness, actionability, presentation evaluated *independently*), a diverse judge panel from 2+ model families, dynamic team selection calibrated on ~20 human-annotated examples, EvalPlanner-style plan-then-execute evaluation, rubric-based scoring with task-specific checklists, and selective escalation to human review for low-confidence cases. This **validates and extends** architectural assumptions.

## Self-improvement loops and rejection libraries are the dominant paradigm

The Rejection Library concept finds strong validation across frontier research. **Hyperagents** (Zhang et al., Meta/UBC, March 2026; arXiv:2603.19461; GitHub: `facebookresearch/Hyperagents`) introduced metacognitive self-modification where the improvement mechanism itself evolves. Paper review scores improved from **0.0 to 0.710**, beating AI-Scientist-v2. The system spontaneously developed persistent memory, performance tracking, and adaptive exploration—behaviors structurally identical to a rejection library. Meta-level improvements transferred across domains and compounded across runs.

The **Darwin Gödel Machine** (Zhang, Hu, Lu, Lange, Clune; arXiv:2505.22954; GitHub: `jennyzzt/dgm`) achieved **2.5× improvement on SWE-bench** (20% → 50%) through evolutionary self-modification with a growing archive of agent variants—structurally analogous to storing rejected attempts with quality signals. **ADAS** (Hu, Lu, Clune, ICLR 2025; arXiv:2408.08435; GitHub: `ShengranHu/ADAS`) defined Automated Design of Agentic Systems, where a meta-agent iteratively programs new agents, tests them, and adds them to an ever-growing archive. Discovered agents transfer across models and domains. The related AFLOW system (also ICLR 2025) outperformed manual methods by **5.7%** and automated approaches by **19.5%**.

**ARTIST** (Singh et al., Microsoft Research, April 2025; arXiv:2505.01441) unified agentic reasoning with outcome-based RL, achieving **+22% absolute improvement** on AMC mathematical competitions—Qwen2.5-14B-ARTIST outperformed GPT-4o. The Comprehensive Survey of Self-Evolving AI Agents (Fang et al., August 2025; arXiv:2508.07407; GitHub: `EvoAgentX/Awesome-Self-Evolving-Agents`) explicitly categorized memory evolution as a core dimension, validating that persistent experience storage is the consensus architecture.

The critical constraint comes from "Mind the Gap" (Song et al., ICLR 2025 Oral; arXiv:2412.02674): **self-improvement saturates after 2–3 rounds without new information**. The generation-verification gap (GV-Gap) determines when self-improvement works—it succeeds when verification is easier than generation. Cross-family verification is especially effective. This means the Rejection Library must continually accumulate new failure patterns to break saturation, and the evaluator must be stronger than the generator.

Prompt evolution is empirically validated: **EvoPrompt** (ICLR 2025) achieved up to **25% improvement on BBH** over human-engineered prompts. **GEPA** (ICLR 2026 Oral) outperformed GRPO by **20% while using 35× fewer rollouts**, demonstrating prompt evolution is highly sample-efficient. RAFT/Reinforce-Rej (Xiong et al., April 2025; arXiv:2504.11343) revealed a critical nuance: **GRPO's main advantage comes from discarding prompts with entirely incorrect responses**, not from reward normalization. Negative samples should be used selectively, not indiscriminately.

**Net conclusion for self-improvement:** The Rejection Library is strongly validated—every top self-improving system uses persistent experience/archive storage. Key design requirements: store structured failure information (failed output, specific evaluator feedback, error classification, task context), maintain diversity in stored failures to prevent local optima, use cross-model verification, expect diminishing returns after 2–3 iterations and inject new signals to break plateaus, and use failures selectively for prompt evolution rather than training on all negatives. This **strongly validates** architectural assumptions.

## Automated research demands DAG structures and citation verification

"Characterizing Deep Research" (Java et al., Microsoft Research, ICLR 2026; arXiv:2508.04183; GitHub: `microsoft/LiveDRBench`) provided the theoretical foundation: deep research is formally a **DAG of information synthesis** where quality depends on search intensity × reasoning intensity, not report length. On LiveDRBench (100 tasks), F1 scores ranged from **0.02 to 0.72**, with OpenAI Deep Research achieving best overall F1 of 0.55. Systems systematically underutilize backtracking and replanning. The separation of claim synthesis from report generation—intermediate claim representations before writing—is a key validated pattern.

DeepResearch Bench (Du et al., ICLR 2026; arXiv:2506.11763; GitHub: `Ayanami0730/deep_research_bench`) benchmarked 100 PhD-level tasks across 22 fields. **Gemini-2.5-Pro leads with RACE score 48.88**, followed by OpenAI Deep Research at 46.98. A stark trade-off emerged: Gemini achieves **111 effective citations** but OpenAI's citation accuracy is only **77.96%** versus Perplexity's **90.24%** with fewer citations. Business & Finance represents the highest proportion of real deep research queries—directly validating the consulting use case.

The AI Scientist (Lu et al., Nature 651, 914–919, March 2026; GitHub: `SakanaAI/AI-Scientist`) demonstrated end-to-end research automation is feasible at ~$6–15 per paper, but independent evaluation revealed a **42% experiment failure rate**, hallucinated citations, misclassification of established concepts as novel, and median of just **5 citations per paper** with most outdated. Quality "resembles a rushed undergraduate paper." AI Scientist-v2 produced the first fully AI-generated paper to pass peer review, but the gap to professional-grade output remains significant.

**STORM** (Shao et al., NAACL 2024; arXiv:2402.14207; GitHub: `stanford-oval/storm`) validated multi-perspective question-asking with a **25% increase in perceived organization** and 10% in breadth. The finding that outline quality strongly predicts final output quality validates the importance of the content structuring phase. **Co-STORM** (Jiang et al., EMNLP 2024) extended this with collaborative human-AI curation: **70% of participants preferred it over search engines**, 78% over RAG chatbots. The moderator agent pattern—proactively finding unexplored information—is critical for comprehensive coverage.

**WebThinker** (Li et al., NeurIPS 2025; arXiv:2504.21776; GitHub: `RUC-NLPIR/WebThinker`) achieved score 8.0, surpassing Gemini Deep Research's 7.9, through interleaved think-search-draft rather than rigid pipelines. **Fathom-DeepResearch** (Singh et al., NeurIPS SEA Workshop; arXiv:2509.24107; GitHub: `FractalAIResearchLabs/Fathom-DeepResearch`) showed that a dual-model architecture (4B search + 4B synthesis) outperforms much larger closed-source agents, with steerable step-level rewards classifying each tool call by cognitive behavior.

**Net conclusion for automated research:** The pipeline is validated with critical additions: claim-level intermediate representations between research and writing, DAG-structured (not linear) research exploration with backtracking, citation verification as a mandatory pipeline stage (hallucination rates of 18–55% across systems make this non-negotiable), and multi-perspective discovery before research begins. This **validates and extends** architectural assumptions.

## Agent isolation prevents error amplification and confirmation bias echo chambers

The Google DeepMind scaling study (Kim, Gu, et al., December 2025; arXiv:2512.08296) is the single most important validation paper for Keystone's architecture. Across **180 controlled configurations**, independent multi-agent systems amplified errors **17.2×** through unchecked propagation. Centralized coordination contained this to **4.4×** and improved performance by **80.9% on parallelizable tasks**. However, **all multi-agent variants degraded performance by 39–70% on sequential reasoning tasks**. A capability saturation threshold emerged at **~45% single-agent accuracy**: beyond this, coordination yields diminishing or negative returns. Agent count saturates at **3–4 agents**.

DPVI maps to the "centralized" topology (parallel generation + centralized verification), not the "independent" topology that suffers 17.2× amplification. This is a critical distinction.

**MAST** (Cemri et al., UC Berkeley, NeurIPS 2025 Spotlight; GitHub: `multi-agent-systems-failure-taxonomy/MAST`) analyzed 1,642 traces across seven MAS frameworks and found **coordination breakdowns account for 36.9% of all failures**—the largest category. Failure rates ranged from **41% to 86.7%** across frameworks. Many failures stem from upstream specification flaws, weak role definitions, and inadequate oversight—directly validating DPVI's specification-driven decomposition.

MoLaCE (Kim & Kim, December 2025; arXiv:2512.23518) demonstrated that **multi-agent debate creates echo chambers** when agents are architecturally similar, amplifying rather than correcting confirmation bias. Liang et al. (EMNLP 2024; GitHub: `Skytliang/Multi-Agents-Debate`) identified the Degeneration-of-Thought problem: once an LLM establishes confidence, self-reflection cannot generate novel thoughts. Independent generation followed by structured synthesis—exactly the DPVI pattern—is superior. Measurement of confirmation bias in LLM code review (arXiv:2603.18740) showed adversarial framing succeeds in **35% of cases against Copilot and 88% against Claude Code**, but debiasing via metadata redaction restores detection in **94%** of cases.

Hierarchical architectures consistently outperform flat ones: **AgentOrchestra** achieved 95.3% on SimpleQA versus 49.4% for a single LLM. Moore's taxonomy survey (arXiv:2508.12683) confirmed "empirical results consistently show hierarchical decompositions outperform flat baselines in stability, data efficiency, and correctness."

**Net conclusion for architecture:** Strict agent isolation during research with centralized verification is the empirically optimal pattern for parallelizable analytical tasks. DPVI is strongly supported, with the specification-driven decomposition phase being particularly critical (MAST shows spec flaws cause cascading failures). The 3–4 agent saturation limit and 45% accuracy threshold are important operational constraints. This **strongly validates** the core architectural choice.

## Specification engines, rejection sampling, and RAG quality complete the picture

**Specification-driven development** has emerged as a major 2025 practice. The SDD paper (Piskala, January 2026; arXiv:2602.00180) formalizes spec-first philosophy with three rigor levels. GitHub's Spec Kit, AWS Kiro, and Augment Code all implement spec-driven workflows in production. The SQUER "Intent Engineering" framework provides the most relevant insight: "AI agents don't need more precise instructions—they need more context and more freedom. Intent hits the sweet spot between requirements (over-constrained) and user stories (under-specified)." McKinsey's 2025 Technology Trends Outlook reports **20–45% developer productivity gains** from spec-driven approaches. The specification engine should capture business intent with rich domain context rather than detailed procedural instructions.

**Rejection sampling** is validated as a cornerstone technique. RSO (ICLR 2024) consistently outperforms SLiC and DPO. The test-time scaling literature (Snell et al., ICLR 2025) proves that Best-of-N sampling with coverage conditions is theoretically optimal when the generation-verification gap is large—directly justifying parallel research agents generating multiple outputs with an evaluator selecting the best. SWIFT (Guo et al., May 2025) learns reward functions from hidden states with **12.7% higher accuracy** than EurusRM-7B using less than 0.005% of parameters.

**Constitutional AI** principles map directly to evaluator design. The C3AI paper (ACM Web Conference 2025) found fine-tuned CAI models adhere better to **negatively framed principles** than positive ones—evaluator rubrics should be primarily phrased as "do not" constraints. Rubrics as Rewards (Scale AI, NeurIPS 2025 Workshop) achieved **28% improvement** using structured checklists with Essential/Important/Optional/Pitfall categories, and "Checklists Are Better Than Reward Models" (Viswanathan et al., 2025) confirmed structured criteria beat opaque reward models.

**Multi-source RAG quality** requires hybrid retrieval and multi-stage verification. MultiRAG (Wu et al., August 2025; arXiv:2508.03553) found **~70% of retrieved paragraphs don't directly contain answers** but include tangentially related information causing misguidance. Three hallucination types dominate: inter-source inconsistency, sparse data distribution, and information conflicts. ReDeEP (ICLR 2025 Spotlight) discovered that RAG hallucinations occur mechanistically when internal knowledge overrides retrieved content. Self-reflective RAG reduces hallucinations to **5.8%** in clinical evaluation, and multi-stage verification frameworks consistently outperform single-pass generation.

## How every finding maps to Keystone's six layers

| Pipeline Layer | Validation Status | Critical Design Change Required |
|---|---|---|
| **Specification Engine** | Validated | Capture intent + context, not procedural instructions; use structured spec format with validation checkpoints |
| **Parallel Research Agents** | Strongly validated | Cap at 3–4 agents; use hybrid retrieval (dense + sparse + KG); implement self-reflective RAG; add citation verification |
| **Multi-Perspective Deliberation** | Conditionally validated | Replace naive debate with structured critique-and-refine; use methodological diversity not persona diversity; one pass with validity-weighted consensus |
| **Content Structuring** | Validated | Use claim-level intermediate representations; DAG structure with backtracking; outline quality predicts output quality |
| **Evaluator** | Validated as most important | Decompose into independent dimensions; use diverse 3+ judge ensemble from 2+ model families; rubric-based with task-specific checklists; escalation for low confidence |
| **Rejection Library** | Strongly validated | Store structured failure data (output + feedback + error class + context); maintain failure diversity; use selectively for prompt evolution; inject new signals to break 2–3 iteration saturation |

## Conclusion

The 2024–2026 literature provides unusually strong convergent evidence for Keystone's architectural choices. The DPVI pattern—though not named in any single paper—emerges as a synthesis of best practices from DeepMind's scaling study (centralized verification prevents 17.2× error amplification), Berkeley's MAST taxonomy (specification flaws cause 36.9% of failures), the confirmation bias literature (isolation prevents echo chambers), and the self-improvement literature (archive-based evolution is the dominant paradigm). Three findings require immediate design attention: deliberation must use structured critique-and-refine with methodological diversity rather than naive debate; the evaluator must decompose into independent dimensions with diverse model families to combat the 12+ identified biases and 60–68% expert-domain agreement ceiling; and the Rejection Library must inject new failure signals to overcome the empirically observed 2–3 iteration saturation limit. The most underappreciated finding is from the DAG formalization of deep research: quality depends on claim-level intermediate representations and search fan-out, not report length—suggesting the content structuring layer should operate on synthesized claims rather than raw agent outputs. No paper contradicts the core architecture; the literature's message is that **quality through structure** is not just a design preference but an empirical necessity.
